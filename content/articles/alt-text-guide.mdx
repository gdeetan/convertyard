## What is alt text and why it matters

The content of the `alt` attribute for HTML image elements is known as the **'alt text'**.

```html
<img src="product-photo.jpg" alt="Blue running shoes with white sole, laces tied" />
```

**Screen reader software** (used by people who are blind or have poor vision) reads out the text in the `alt` attribute instead of describing the image to the user. If no alt text is provided, then the screen reader would typically announce 'image' or read out the filename of the image (e.g. `product-photo-FINAL-v3.jpg`).

Beyond screen readers, alt text matters for:

**Search engine indexing.** As Google cannot 'read' images of web pages, they use the alt text to determine what an image is featuring. Well-written alt text for product images not only means that the page will be **more relevant**, but also that images will **rank better in image searches**.

**Failed image loads.** The browser may display the alt text for an image that fails to load from the web (due to a slow connection, for example) or where the URL for the image has been mistyped or has changed.

**Cognitive accessibility.** Users with cognitive disabilities may find images confusing or even distracting. Adding accurate alt text lets them read the information the image was trying to convey.

## The EAA and WCAG requirements

The **European Accessibility Act** (Directive 2019/882) was implemented on **28 June 2025**. The Directive applies to companies that sell goods and/or services to consumers within the EU. The scope of the Directive is very broad and includes: **e-commerce, banking, transport services, media services, and consumer product electronics**. The requirements for accessibility have to be met by ensuring that services provided to customers meet the **WCAG 2.1 Level AA** accessibility requirements.

According to the **WCAG 2.1 criterion 1.1.1 Non-text Content**, all non-text content must have a text equivalent that can convey the same message to users. This applies to images as well and should be provided as alt text. The only cases where no text equivalent is needed are: 1) **decorative images** that have no function, for which `alt=""` is required; 2) **CAPTCHA images**, for which an alternative method must be provided to validate the user; 3) **tests and exercises** for which a text description would defeat the purpose.

In the **U.S.**, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) does not specifically mention websites. Since the Supreme Court has declined to hear **Robles v. Domino's Pizza in 2019**, most courts are following the opinion of the 9th Circuit that application of the ADA to business websites is proper. Web content on all federal, state and local government websites must conform to WCAG 2.1 AA standards under **Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act**.

In the **UK**, the **Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018** require public sector websites to meet the accessibility requirements outlined in WCAG 2.1 AA. Private sector organizations are subject to similar accessibility requirements under the **Equality Act 2010**.

**Practical implication:** If your organization sells online to customers in the EU, provides services to customers or the public or creates content with statutory requirements, then **all meaningful images on your web site will need to have alt text**. "We'll add it later" is no longer a valid position.

## How to write good alt text

Good alt text is **specific, concise and attempts to describe the visual part of content within context**.

### Informative images

For photos and illustrations that convey information:

- Describe **what is visually present**, not what you wish were there
- Include **color, action, composition, and text** that is shown in the image in your description
- Keep it **under 125 characters** where possible
- Don't start with "Image of", "Photo of" etc. Screen readers already announce "image" so it's redundant

**Instead of:** `alt="meeting"`
**Write:** `alt="Four people seated around a conference table reviewing printed documents"`

**Instead of:** `alt="chart"`
**Write:** `alt="Bar chart showing monthly revenue increasing from $12k in January to $47k in June 2025"`

### Functional images (buttons, links, icons)

For images that perform an action when clicked:

- **Action, not image**: The action that will occur when the image is clicked. `alt="Search"` not `alt="Magnifying glass"`
- **Icon-only buttons**: `alt="Close"`, `alt="Menu"`, `alt="Share"`, `alt="Download"`

### Decorative images

If an image is just for looks and does not add any meaning or help explain the page, the image should have an **empty alt attribute**:

```html
<img src="decorative-squiggle.svg" alt="" />
```

This tells people who use screen readers to skip the image. **Do not leave out the `alt` attribute**, because if you do, screen readers will read out the file name.

### Product images

For e-commerce:

- Add the **product name, main features, and color**
- Help the shopper **know what the product is without seeing** the product
- `alt="Nike Air Max 90 sneaker in white and grey, right shoe from the side"`

## Bad vs. good alt text examples

| Image | Bad alt text | Good alt text |
|---|---|---|
| Portrait photo | `photo.jpg` | `Marketing director Sarah Chen smiling in a white blazer against a grey background` |
| Product shot | `product` | `Stainless steel water bottle, 32oz, matte black finish with push-button lid` |
| Bar chart | `chart showing data` | `Bar chart: website traffic grew from 8,000 to 24,000 monthly visitors, Jan–Jun 2025` |
| Logo in nav | `logo` | `ConvertYard — return to homepage` |
| Decorative divider | `divider line` | `` (empty alt attribute) |
| Screenshot | `screenshot` | `Firefox browser showing the ConvertYard homepage with a file dropzone in the center` |
