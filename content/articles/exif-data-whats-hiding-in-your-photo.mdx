## The Photo You Just Sent May Contain Your Home Address

Photos taken with location services enabled on your smartphone have **the GPS coordinates where the photo was taken recorded by the camera**. These are stored as EXIF metadata in the JPEG files created by your camera. When you share these files by email, via a direct link, via AirDrop, or in a forum, the coordinates are shared with the file. **Anyone with the file can extract them.**

This is the default behavior for all smartphone cameras that have location services enabled, including Apple Camera, Google Pixel Camera, and Samsung Camera. The only ways to prevent location from being embedded in a JPEG file are to **disable location services for the camera app** (or set it to "Ask Each Time" and then deny it) or switch location services off for the app in Settings.

Open the image file's properties on any computer, find the GPS fields, and paste them into a map. That's all. No special software or special knowledge of image properties is needed.

## What EXIF Data Contains

<ExifDataMapIllustration />

EXIF (Exchangeable Image File Format) is a standard for images containing metadata within JPEG and TIFF files. Often used within photographs shot with cameras, many modern smartphones contain EXIF data also within images shot with the phone.

A typical photo from a modern smartphone contains:

- **GPS coordinates**: The photo's GPS coordinates are recorded to the exact meter where the photo was taken.
- **Timestamp**: Date and time of image capture. Even the time zone is included.
- **Camera make and model**: "Apple iPhone 15 Pro", "Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra", "Google Pixel 9"
- **Exposure settings**: aperture (f-stop), shutter speed, ISO sensitivity
- **Lens information**: The image-capturing lens used (e.g., 28mm, 12.2mm, 2.5x, 75mm). The equivalent focal length and optical zoom factor are typically also included in the EXIF data.
- **Orientation**: This field describes how the camera was held when taking the photo. The orientation can be portrait, landscape, or even upside-down.
- **Software**: The software package used to edit pictures after they were taken (e.g. Lightroom, Snapseed, …).
- **Flash status**: fired or not and in what mode (e.g. auto, off, fill).
- **White balance**: in auto mode or a fixed mode (with temperature in Kelvin).

Some of this is neutral. For example, knowing the camera model and exposure settings can be very useful for a photographer. Also, knowing the image's orientation will help any app display it correctly (i.e., not upside down).

**GPS coordinates are the key item of interest here.**

## A Real Scenario

You take a picture of something at home (e.g., a sofa you want to sell, a contract you need to share, a product you are adding to your online shop) and upload the pictures to a website to share with the world or send to someone you haven't met before.

The original photo file is shared with anyone who can view it. This can be downloaded by anyone viewing the image and opened on their computer. With a right-click on the image in the opened file, the properties can be viewed in the Details tab. The GPS coordinates for the location where the image was taken are copied from here and then pasted into Google Maps or Apple Maps.

**It is that street that you live on. Often it is that very building.**

This is how many journalists and security researchers have been able to reverse engineer location information from shared photos, over and over again. A camera with location services enabled, a file shared directly rather than through a metadata-stripping platform, and coordinates extracted by the recipient from the shared file.

The greatest risk comes with **direct file sharing** (e.g., attaching a file to an email, using AirDrop, uploading to file hosting services like Google Drive or WeTransfer, sharing images on forums and communities that serve the original, unprocessed images).

Most social media sites (Facebook, Instagram, X/Twitter) strip EXIF from uploaded photos. But be aware that such site policy can change at any time. A photo of furniture you are selling can travel as an email attachment or via AirDrop and contain location data, while the same photo posted on Facebook by you would not.

## How to Check What's in Your Photo

Verify the content of your photos' metadata before you share them with the following three methods:

**On a Mac**: Open the photo in Preview. Go to Tools > Show Inspector (or use the shortcut ⌘I). Then click on Exif to view the photo's EXIF information. If your photo contains GPS information, you will find the "Latitude" and "Longitude" fields and enter decimal values there.

**On Windows**: Right-click on a file in Windows Explorer and choose Properties. Go to the Details tab and scroll down to the bottom of the screen. Click on the field heading to collapse the section, and then click on 'GPS' to collapse that section further. If you see 'GPS Latitude' and 'GPS Longitude' fields with decimal values in them, then the file contains your location data.

**Via ConvertYard**: Just drop your image files into the [compress image](/compress-image/) tool. In the analyzer panel on the right side of the app, the EXIF summary for each image is automatically analyzed for you, and **GPS coordinates are flagged with a warning before you compress the images**. This makes it easy to review many images one at a time.

If the file does have GPS, then the location will be visible in the GPS coordinates at the time the photo was taken, and you can decide whether to share the image or not.

## Stripping EXIF Data

Two straightforward options:

**Strip and keep the image format**: Use Compress Image. Uncheck "Include metadata" in the settings panel and select "Remove metadata" from the dropdown list. **No quality loss, just no EXIF data in the output images.** The pixel data remains unchanged, and no metadata is appended to the output images.

Open the newly downloaded file in Preview and check Properties again. If GPS metadata was in the file before, it should not be there anymore. If the file does not have any EXIF metadata at all, including no GPS metadata, then that was successful as well. The EXIF tab may not even appear in Preview's Inspector if there is no metadata.

**Convert the format**: A JPEG file converted to WebP using [jpg-to-webp](/jpg-to-webp/) will be a WebP file. WebP supports Exif metadata. However, many converters do not copy the metadata by default. The created WebP file will most likely not contain any location data. So verify the EXIF as well by opening the created file in Preview and then check the location in the Inspector under EXIF.

Both of these methods are really quick and simple to do and can be of value when sharing photos taken at home, regular locations, or anywhere else you might not want others to know you were.

## When to Keep EXIF Data

While the EXIF metadata of photos can be a liability in many situations, there are also situations in which it is advantageous to retain this metadata.

**For photography portfolios**, it is important to preserve the technical metadata (aperture, shutter speed, ISO value, focal length, etc.). In order to find out what you did in order to achieve certain results while taking photos of your work, the EXIF data is very helpful. Potential clients also like to see the technical approach a photographer uses and thus preserve the corresponding data as well.

**Archival use**: There are times when you want to keep timestamp and camera information in the EXIF data for cataloging purposes.

**Stock photo submission**: In case the images are going to a stock agency, the copyright information and the technical details can be of value. Check the requirements of the platforms you are submitting to.

The decision to strip your EXIF data from your photos depends on the recipient of your photo and the things that they can do with the metadata that you strip off. For stock agencies or photo archives, you want to keep the full EXIF data (GPS, etc.) for them. For publishing your photos on a website (in a gallery, etc.) or sending off to strangers, **remove the GPS fields (at a minimum)**.

The pixel data in the images remains the same. In short, removing the EXIF data has no effect on the images themselves.
