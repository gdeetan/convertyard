## The Format Question Nobody Gives a Straight Answer To

"Which image format should I use?" Oh, "it depends" on too many variables. But the calculations changed when **AVIF** stopped being an experiment and started to gain broader support. Here is my take on the matter, updated for 2026.

## What Each Format Optimizes For

**JPEG** (1992): The **universal base image format**. Lossy, no transparency. Maximum compatibility with every device, OS, browser, email program, government website, and old piece of software from the last 30 years or so. JPEG files are bigger than equivalent quality images in other formats, but the massive compatibility advantage makes them the best choice for the baseline image.

**WebP** (2010, Google): A JPEG alternative. WebP is about **30% to 90% smaller** than JPEG-optimized images while looking almost identically good. WebP supports both lossy and lossless compression as well as transparent images with an alpha channel. It has gotten excellent browser support, and all major browsers have supported it since 2020.

**AVIF** (2019, AV1 video codec-based): AVIF is currently the **best in terms of compression**, beating WebP in size by a factor of **3–60%** for similar image quality. It is particularly good at compressing images containing gradients, fine details, as well as images shot in high dynamic range. AVIF supports both lossy and lossless compression and also supports full transparency, just like WebP.

## Browser Support in 2026

AVIF has reached mainstream browser support:

- **Chrome**: supported since August 2020
- **Firefox**: supported since October 2021
- **Safari**: AVIF support is included in Safari 16.4 or later on macOS Ventura 13.3 or iOS 16.4 or later. You can [convert AVIF animated content to GIF](/avif-to-gif/) for use in older email clients, Slack messages, GitHub comments, and other web locations where animated AVIF is not supported.
- **Edge**: supported since version 121 (January 2024)
- **Samsung Internet**: supported

According to caniuse.com, the **global browser support for AVIF is currently higher than 94%** and has been for the last couple of years. The missing 6% or so of global browser support for AVIF likely falls into the category of iOS 15 and earlier, on iPhone 6s and later, plus some older iPads that cannot be upgraded past iOS 15, and a very small number of older browsers used in very old and legacy corporate environments.

WebP has around **97–98% browser support**. Note that the list of excluded browsers is slightly shorter than for AVIF, but otherwise similar.

JPEG: **100% everywhere**.

## Compression at a Glance

For a typical high-quality photograph at similar perceived quality:

| Format | Relative file size | Transparency | Lossless | Universal compatibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| JPEG | 100% (baseline) | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |
| WebP | ~65–75% of JPEG | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (all modern browsers) |
| AVIF | ~45–60% of JPEG | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (~94% coverage) |

This is approximate. **Natural images** (e.g. scenes) generally compress better than **geometric images** (e.g. charts and graphs). High-quality originals generally save more than heavily compressed JPEGs.

## One Genuine Trade-Off: Encoding Speed

Note that the time it takes to **encode an AVIF image is significantly greater** than for JPEG and WebP images. While the difference is not noticeable when encoding a single image on the fly, for batch encoding thousands of images at build time or on a server, the difference can be quite significant. For high-quality images, the AVIF encoder can be **5–10 times slower** than the WebP encoder, depending on the specific encoder and quality settings.

If images are encoded in a build process, AVIF at high quality might even slow down the build. **WebP at comparable quality settings is then often a good trade-off** between image quality and encoding time.

You don't need to worry about decoding speed for display, as both AVIF and WebP decode very quickly on modern hardware.

## When to Pick Each Format

**Use AVIF when:**
- Serve images on websites, delivered via the `<picture>` element with WebP or JPEG fallbacks
- **Web performance is the primary objective**, and you want the smallest possible files to achieve the best results
- Your audience consists mostly of modern devices and browsers (2021 and newer)
- Your new website will be optimized for the latest browsers

**Use WebP when:**
- Your need for a **single modern image format with almost complete support** is greater than your needs
- Your current pipeline already supports WebP and switching to AVIF would require changes to your processing
- You need **transparency with broad browser reach**
- Your primary use case is to generate images very fast, but you cannot use AVIF for some reason

**Use JPEG when:**
- **Unknown recipient** (e.g., via email attachment, file transfer, external sharing)
- The target is a government portal, print service, stock photo library, or legacy system
- You need **maximum compatibility without a fallback strategy**
- The image has no transparency requirements

## Quick Decision Framework

```
Serving images on a website?
  Yes → AVIF with WebP fallback via <picture> element
  No → Does it need transparency?
       Yes → WebP (or PNG for lossless)
       No → JPEG
```

Email, attached files to forms, government portals, old print services, and legacy applications — all of these require **JPEG** for maximum compatibility without the risk of things not working.

For your website images (that you control) to run on the smallest files possible on modern browsers while staying fully backward compatible with older ones: **AVIF + WebP fallback**.
